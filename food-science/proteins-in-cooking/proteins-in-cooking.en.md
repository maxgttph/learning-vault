# Proteins in Cooking

## TL;DR

> A native protein is a folded ball that hides its hydrophobic patches. Everything it can do in the kitchen — emulsify, foam, bind, curdle — follows from a single move: **opening up**. Opened against a free interface, it protects and stabilises; opened among other opened proteins, it aggregates and coagulates. It is a **bell curve**, and knowing how to cook is knowing where to stop on it.

## Key concepts

- **Denaturation** — loss of a protein's folded structure under heat, whisking, acid or salt. Buried hydrophobic zones become accessible.
- **Coagulation / aggregation** — unfolded proteins bond to each other into a network. **Irreversible**: you cannot "un-cook" an egg.
- **Syneresis** — contraction of the protein network, expelling the water it was holding. The custard that "weeps", the whey escaping the curd.
- **Globular protein** — a compact, ordered ball (egg white, many plant proteins). A poor cold emulsifier.
- **Flexible protein** — a loose, disordered structure with hydrophobic zones already accessible (caseins, yolk lipoproteins). Emulsifies cold.
- **Casein micelle** — a spherical aggregate of milk proteins, kept in suspension by a charged kappa-casein "hair".

## Deep dive

### Why unfolding makes a protein an emulsifier

A native protein is badly equipped for an oil-water interface: its hydrophobic amino acids are carefully buried in the core of the structure, its hydrophilic ones turned towards the water. In that compact form it has nothing to offer a fat droplet.

**Partial** unfolding — gentle heat, whisking, a little acid or salt — loosens the ball and exposes those hydrophobic zones. The protein then becomes operationally amphiphilic: patches that dive into the oil, patches that stay in the water. It adsorbs at the interface, spreads out, and forms a **viscoelastic film** — a genuine protein net around the droplet. An excellent steric stabiliser (see [[emulsions.en]]).

This principle is exploited everywhere: you whip egg whites to unfold them mechanically so they stabilise air bubbles; in a mayonnaise, whisking and the vinegar's acidity partially denature the yolk proteins and make them more effective. **Controlled denaturation = better emulsifier.**

### Where it tips over: the bell curve

The problem comes from those very exposed hydrophobic patches. As long as they are pressed against an oil droplet, all is well. But if there are **more unfolded proteins than available interface**, or if the unfolding is so brutal that proteins meet each other in solution before reaching a droplet, then the patches stick **to each other** rather than to the oil. Protein-to-protein instead of protein-to-interface: that is the start of aggregation, then of coagulation into granules.

Three dials set the tipping point:

1. **Intensity and duration of heating.** Gentle and brief → useful unfolding. Strong and prolonged → total unfolding, high mobility, frequent collisions, patches welded to each other. The denaturation temperature depends on the protein (often $60$–$80\ °\text{C}$); aggregation follows shortly after.
2. **Interface / protein ratio.** Plenty of interface to cover (fine, numerous droplets) → every unfolded protein finds a spot and behaves. Too many proteins for the available interface → the surplus aggregates.
3. **The environment.** Salt, acid and calcium **screen the electrostatic repulsions** between proteins and let them come closer — hence they promote aggregation. That is why coconut milk acidified with lime and boiled hard curdles far faster than coconut milk that is merely heated.

> **The image to keep.** Unfolding a protein means opening its arms. Open arms against an oil droplet = a protective embrace, it emulsifies. Open arms meeting other open arms in solution = they grab each other, it precipitates into grains. The dividing line: *is there a free interface to seize at the moment the protein opens?*

### Two families of emulsifying proteins

- **Type A — flexible, already "open" → emulsify cold.** A loose, disordered structure with hydrophobic zones partly accessible without heating. They plaster themselves onto the interface immediately, at room temperature. *Examples: milk caseins, egg yolk lipoproteins.* That is why mayonnaise is built cold.
- **Type B — globular, tightly folded → become emulsifiers by opening.** Compact balls that hide their hydrophobic zones well; mediocre in native form. They need unfolding (gentle heat, whisking, acid). *Examples: egg white proteins, many plant proteins.*

The bell curve mainly concerns type B: partial unfolding = great emulsifier, excessive unfolding = destructive aggregation. Type A, already open, gains nothing from heat — and degrades too if pushed.

### The five roles of protein in a sauce

Proteins are Swiss army knives. Depending on their structure and the conditions:

1. **Emulsify** — sit at the oil-water interface and stabilise droplets with a steric film. The yolk in a mayonnaise, the coconut proteins in a curry.
2. **Foam** — same principle, but the interface is **air-water**. Whipped whites, mousses, sabayons.
3. **Bind / thicken / give body** — by coagulating, they form a network that structures the dish. Hard-boiled egg, flan, set custard, quiche, tofu, crème anglaise, the body of cooked meat. **Here coagulation is wanted and controlled.**
4. **Flavour and colour** — heated dry with sugars, their amino acids feed the **Maillard reaction** (see [[fats-heat-and-aroma.en]]). The protein is no longer structural, it is an aroma precursor.
5. **Nourish and give depth** — the amino acids, including the glutamate responsible for **umami**, bring the savoury depth of a long-simmered stock.

The first three roles all rest on the ability to open up. The "body" role comes precisely from coagulation; the "emulsion" role comes from adsorption at the interface *before* it coagulates. It is all a matter of degree and timing: **stop early and it emulsifies and foams; push a bit and it binds; push too far and it curdles; heat it dry and it perfumes.**

### Beautiful gel or granules? The same reaction, two regimes

The difference between a silky set custard and a split sauce is not the nature of the phenomenon — it is always coagulation — but its **degree and evenness**. A network that forms slowly and regularly traps the water and gives a smooth gel. A network that contracts too fast and too hard **expels** the water (syneresis) and tightens into grains.

### What the granules are made of, and the separated phases

When a sauce truly splits, you get not two but **three** materials, sorted by affinity and by density:

- **The granules** ≈ coagulated proteins + trapped fat + insoluble solids. The core is a protein agglomerate; at the moment the film falls off, part of the oil stays imprisoned in the mesh (hence a granule that feels greasy, never dry); ground peanut fibres, coconut pulp fragments and gelatinised starch cling to it. Sometimes protein-polyphenol complexes (with tomato) or calcium-bridged proteins are added in, stiffening the clump.
- **The fat phase** (on top): coalesced free triglycerides, and everything fat-soluble dissolved in it — pigments (the red-orange carotenoids of tomato and palm oil, hence the colour of a mafé's slick), fatty aromas, vitamins A, D, E, K.
- **The aqueous phase** (underneath): water, salts, sugars, acids, water-soluble aromas, still-soluble proteins that have not coagulated, dispersed starch, fine particles in suspension.

It is exactly the opposite of the emulsifier's work, which reconciled these three worlds. When it falls, each goes home.

> **Kitchen rule of thumb.** A smooth oil slick with no grain → pure coalescence, recoverable by re-dispersing with liquid. Visible lumps → the proteins have coagulated: irreversible for the granules themselves, but you can rebind the sauce around them, and fine granules go unnoticed once stirred or blended.

### Milk curdling and cheese: aggregating without heat

Milk is both an emulsion (dispersed fat globules) and a suspension (casein micelles). Curdling it means destabilising the micelles so they aggregate into a solid gel — the **curd** — which separates from the **whey**. Two routes, and they illuminate everything else:

**The acid route** (yoghurt, fresh cheeses, ricotta, milk going off). Casein micelles are normally negatively charged and repel one another — exactly the electrostatic stabilisation mechanism of emulsions. Acidifying (lemon, vinegar, or lactic fermentation) neutralises those charges. No more repulsion → the micelles aggregate and the milk sets.

**The enzymatic route** (rennet, for most ripened cheeses). A new, chemical and targeted mechanism: **chymosin** acts as a molecular scissor and cuts precisely the **kappa-casein**, the small protein that formed a protective "hair" on the micelle surface keeping them apart. With the hair cut, the micelles aggregate — this time via **calcium bridges**, which gives a more elastic and stronger gel than the acid one. Hence cheeses that hold together, slice and ripen.

Then, common to both routes: draining. The whey escapes by **syneresis** — the protein network contracts and expels water, exactly the phenomenon that makes an overcooked flan weep. Pressing, salting and ripening follow.

### The full typology of protein aggregation

```
Four triggers, one destination:
proteins that stop repelling each other, aggregate into a network,
then trap and finally release water.

  HEAT       unfolding → hydrophobic patches stuck   cooked egg, over-simmered mafé
  ACID       charge neutralisation                   soured milk, yoghurt, ricotta
  ENZYME     targeted cutting of the shield          rennet cheese
  SALT / Ca²⁺ screening of repulsions (co-factor)    speeds up the other three
```

Curdling is therefore **not** thermal denaturation. It is mechanistically a cousin of coagulation, but triggered differently: by loss of stabilisation, not by thermal unfolding.

## Examples & analogies

- **Open arms.** All of protein cookery fits in that image: a protein that opens up is looking for something to embrace. If an oil droplet is available, it protects it. Otherwise it embraces its neighbour — and that is the lump.
- **The same egg, four jobs.** Whipped cold it foams (stiff whites); its yolk emulsifies (mayonnaise); gently heated it binds (crème anglaise); heated hard and dry it browns and perfumes (Maillard). One molecule, four stages of opening.
- **The reversibility test as a diagnosis.** Granules that re-disperse under the whisk were never coagulated proteins — they were fat. Coagulation cannot be undone: you cannot un-cook an egg.

## Open questions

- Collagen, a case apart: it does not coagulate, it **converts into gelatin** by slow hydrolysis. See [[fats-heat-and-aroma.en]] (confit).
- Why do some proteins gel cold after heating (gelatin) and others not? Thermo-reversible vs thermo-irreversible gels.
- Gluten: a protein network built **mechanically** (kneading) rather than thermally — yet a fifth route to aggregation.
- Why does ricotta combine heat *and* acid, when either route suffices on its own?
