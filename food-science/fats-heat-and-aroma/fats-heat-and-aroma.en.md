# Fats, Heat and Aroma

## TL;DR

> "Fat" is a catch-all word worth unpacking: fatty acid → triglyceride → lipid. And fat plays three thermally distinct roles in cooking — a **searing medium** that lets you reach the $140\ °\text{C}$ of the Maillard reaction, a **gentle cooking bath** that slowly converts collagen into gelatin (confit), and a **solvent** that extracts the fat-soluble aromas of spices. Three temperatures, three chemistries.

## Key concepts

- **Lipid** — the broadest scientific term: anything fatty or oily (triglycerides, phospholipids, sterols, fat-soluble vitamins).
- **Fatty acid** — the basic building block: a long carbon chain ending in an acid group.
- **Triglyceride** — three fatty acids esterified onto a glycerol backbone. This is ordinary dietary fat.
- **Phospholipid** — glycerol + two fatty acids + a polar phosphate head. Amphiphilic, hence emulsifying (lecithin).
- **Maillard reaction** — a covalent reaction between an amino acid and a reducing sugar, dry and around $140$–$165\ °\text{C}$. It creates brown melanoidins and hundreds of aromas.
- **Blooming / lipid extraction** — dissolving spices' fat-soluble aroma molecules into hot fat.

## Deep dive

### The vocabulary of fat, from general to molecular

**Level 1 — the fatty acid.** A long carbon chain with an acid group at one end. Two criteria characterise it:

- **Saturation.** A **saturated** fatty acid has only single bonds between carbons: the chain is **straight**, the molecules stack well, so it is **solid** at room temperature (butter, beef fat, coconut oil). An **unsaturated** fatty acid carries one or more double bonds that create **kinks**: the molecules stack badly, so it is **liquid** (vegetable oils). Mono-unsaturated = one kink (olive oil); poly-unsaturated = several (sunflower, rapeseed, omega-3 and -6).
- **Chain length** — short, medium or long; it influences melting point and digestion.

That geometry explains why coconut oil sets, why butter is hard in the fridge, and why a mayonnaise that has got cold turns grainy: its triglycerides have **crystallised**.

**Level 2 — the triglyceride.** In nature fatty acids almost never travel alone: they are assembled in threes on a glycerol backbone. A cooking oil or fat is essentially a mixture of triglycerides with varied fatty acids. When you say "fat" in the kitchen, you almost always mean triglycerides.

**Level 3 — the cousins that are not triglycerides.** The term "lipid" also covers:

- **phospholipids** — glycerol again, but only two fatty acids and a polar phosphate head. Hence their amphiphilic nature and emulsifying power: egg yolk lecithin, the heart of a mayonnaise, is a *modified* lipid. See [[emulsions.en]];
- **sterols** (cholesterol, phytosterols);
- **fat-soluble vitamins** (A, D, E, K) and **fat-soluble pigments** (carotenoids — the red-orange that migrates into the oil of a mafé).

In the kitchen, finally, we distinguish **fat** (solid at room temperature, rather saturated) from **oil** (liquid, rather unsaturated): the same vocabulary applied to physical state. With this grid you can say precisely "butter is rich in short-chain saturated fatty acids, hence solid and with a low smoke point" rather than "it's fat".

### The Maillard reaction: the real chemical break

Careful, this is a change of register. Denaturation and coagulation (see [[proteins-in-cooking.en]]) belong to **physics and weak interactions**: proteins change shape and stick together, but remain chemically the same molecules. Maillard is **covalent chemistry**: molecules are destroyed and recombined into new ones. You are no longer rearranging, you are transforming.

How it goes: an amino acid's amine group reacts with a reducing sugar's carbonyl (glucose, fructose), forms an unstable compound that rearranges, fragments and recombines into a cascade of hundreds of new molecules — the **melanoidins** (brown pigments) and a host of volatile compounds (grilled, roasted, meaty, nutty).

Two conditions, one of which explains an entire branch of culinary technique:

- **High heat** — Maillard starts significantly around $140$–$165\ °\text{C}$, hence **above the boiling point of water**.
- **Little water** — this is the crucial point. As long as water remains, temperature is capped at $100\ °\text{C}$: boiling water carries the energy away as latent heat. Reaching $150\ °\text{C}$ is impossible. **Boiled meat never browns; seared meat does.**

Hence the universal sequence "sear dry in fat, then add liquid". In a mafé, the base of meat and onions browned before the liquid goes in *is* the dish's dose of Maillard: that is where the deep brown aroma molecules are created, and the later simmering — capped at $100\ °\text{C}$ — merely spreads them around. Throwing everything into water from the start would give a flat dish. Fat here serves as a **searing medium**: it transmits heat well beyond $100\ °\text{C}$, which water cannot do.

### Confit: the exact thermal opposite

To confit is to cook food **immersed in fat, at low temperature and for a long time**. Yes, you have to heat — but gently: typically $60$–$90\ °\text{C}$, far below the Maillard threshold. The goal is not browning. Fat is no longer a searing agent but a **cooking medium**: a bath that transmits gentle, even heat, and excludes water and air.

On the protein side:

- **collagen** (the connective tissue of a duck leg, a shank) slowly denatures and **converts into gelatin** at those low temperatures held for a long time. That is what makes confit meat melting: tough collagen becomes silky gelatin;
- **muscle fibres** denature gently: they barely firm up, stay tender and keep their water, because you never exceed the temperature at which they contract abruptly and squeeze out their juice (which is what happens over high heat);
- **neither coagulation into granules nor Maillard**: at $70\ °\text{C}$ in fat, no browning and no lumps.

Why fat specifically? Three reasons: it **excludes oxygen and water** (the food does not oxidise, does not dry out, keeps — that was the historical purpose); it transmits **very even, gentle** heat, a better thermal vehicle than an air oven for staying low and steady; and it **captures the fat-soluble aroma compounds** of the food and the aromatics, which come back to perfume the flesh.

```
Three thermal regimes of fat

  60–90 °C    CONFIT      collagen → gelatin, no browning
  ~100 °C     (water's ceiling: none of this is possible)
  140–165 °C  MAILLARD    amino acid + sugar → melanoidins + aromas
```

### Why spices give more in fat

The phenomenon is called **lipid extraction of aromas** — in the kitchen, *blooming*, "sweating" or "frying off" spices. The principle follows the same thread as emulsions: *like dissolves like*, polar substances dissolve in polar solvents (water), non-polar ones in non-polar solvents (fat).

Now, a large share of spices' aroma molecules — volatile compounds, essential oils, oleoresins: capsaicin in chilli, curcumin in turmeric, piperine in pepper, terpenes in cumin and cinnamon — are **fat-soluble**, hence non-polar or barely polar. Consequently:

- **in water**, they dissolve badly; the hydrophobic effect (the very one that separates oil from vinegar) keeps them at bay, and much of the aroma stays trapped in the spice particle;
- **in hot fat**, they dissolve abundantly. The fat extracts them from the spice matrix, puts them into solution, makes them available to the whole dish — and available to be perceived in the mouth, where fat coats the palate and prolongs the aroma.

Heat plays a double role: it increases solubility and diffusion (molecules leave the spice faster), and it triggers reactions creating **new** aromas (a bloomed cumin or mustard seed develops notes it does not have raw). But too hot burns the fragile compounds and turns bitter — hence the rule: medium heat, a few tens of seconds, until it smells wonderful.

That is exactly what a Thai curry does when you fry the paste in the coconut oil released after deliberately breaking the coconut cream: you extract the fat-soluble aromas **before** adding liquid. Same logic as the mafé — **aroma chemistry happens in the fat, before the water.**

## Examples & analogies

- **Fat as a solvent, not as an ingredient.** Three of its major culinary functions are those of a medium: a thermal vehicle above $100\ °\text{C}$ (searing), a protective bath below (confit), an extraction solvent (blooming). The flavour it contributes in its own right often comes afterwards.
- **Straight or kinked.** The whole difference between butter and olive oil comes down to the geometry of a chain: straight, it stacks and sets; kinked, it stacks badly and flows. The same geometry explains the grainy mayonnaise out of the fridge.
- **Water caps the temperature.** This is the key linking simmering, searing and confit: as long as there is free water, you are capped at $100\ °\text{C}$ and no browning chemistry is possible. Drying is a prerequisite, not a detail.

## Open questions

- The **smoke point**: where exactly does it come from (free fatty acids, impurities) and why does it vary so much between refined and unrefined oils?
- **Oxidation and rancidity**: why do polyunsaturates go rancid faster? The role of double bonds and antioxidants.
- **Caramelisation** (sugars alone, no amino acid): how does it really differ from Maillard, and at what temperatures?
- **Sous-vide** cooking at low temperature: the same thermal logic as confit, without the fat bath — what is lost?
