# Thickeners and Starch

## TL;DR

> Thickening and emulsifying are two independent operations that are often confused. **Emulsifying** = reconciling fat and water with a film at the interface. **Thickening** = slowing the flow of one phase by tangling long macromolecules into it. A sauce that coats the spoon is a **thickened** sauce, not necessarily an emulsified one — and starch, which is not an emulsifier, nevertheless stabilises emulsions indirectly, by immobilising the droplets.

## Key concepts

- **Viscosity** — a fluid's resistance to flow. That is what thickening increases; no volume is "filled".
- **Starch** — a glucose polymer stored in granules. Two forms: **amylose** (linear chains) and **amylopectin** (highly branched chains).
- **Gelatinisation** — around $62$–$70\ °\text{C}$, the granules absorb water and swell massively. This is the engine of thickening.
- **Retrogradation** — on cooling, amylose re-associates and sets the sauce into a firmer gel.
- **Slurry** — starch dispersed in a little **cold** liquid before incorporation, to separate the granules and avoid lumps.
- **Gum** — a thickening polysaccharide (xanthan, gum arabic, mustard mucilage), active cold and stable to heat.

## Deep dive

### A thickener does not "take up the space"

The spontaneous image — the thickener filling the gaps in the liquid — is wrong, and the intuition that there are no gaps in a liquid is the right one. A thickener **increases resistance to flow**, nothing more.

The real mechanism: long molecules (starch chains, gums) or swollen granules unfold in the water. Those filaments **tangle** with one another and cling to water molecules through hydrogen bonds. The water is still there, in the same quantity, no gap has been filled — but it is no longer free to flow: it is held in a mesh.

The right image is therefore not "filling a hole" but **"tangling and holding back"**. Think of the difference between pure water and water in which kilometres of tangled microscopic spaghetti have been dissolved.

### How cornflour thickens

Cornflour is pure maize starch: microscopic granules of amylose and amylopectin. Cold, those granules are insoluble and hard — thrown into cold water they form a cloudy suspension and nothing more. **Everything happens with heat.**

1. Around $62$–$70\ °\text{C}$, the granules absorb water massively and **swell** — up to several tens of times their volume. Swollen granules pressed against each other obstruct flow: viscosity climbs. That is the main engine.
2. Simultaneously, **amylose leaks** out of the granules and disperses, forming a network that traps the liquid. Swelling + amylose network = the sauce coats.
3. On cooling, amylose re-associates (**retrogradation**) and sets the sauce into a firmer gel. Hence a cornflour sauce that keeps thickening after it leaves the heat.

Two practical consequences follow directly from this mechanism:

**Why you slake it cold.** Thrown dry into a hot liquid, the powder's surface granules gelatinise instantly and form a sticky shell around a core of dry powder: lumps. Dispersed first in a little cold water (a **slurry**), the granules are separated and then swell one by one.

**Why you should not over-boil or over-acidify.** Prolonged boiling or violent stirring **tears** the swollen granules: they empty out, the network loosens, the sauce thins again. Acidity does worse — it **hydrolyses** the starch, cuts the chains and destroys the thickening power. Hence the rule: add cornflour at the end of cooking, let it barely simmer, just enough to gelatinise, and avoid a hard acidic boil.

### Thickening is not emulsifying

The distinction worth engraving:

|  | **Emulsify** | **Thicken** |
|---|---|---|
| Problem solved | Making two mutually fleeing liquids cohabit | Making one liquid more substantial |
| Mechanism | Emulsifier film **at the** oil-water **interface** | Tangled macromolecules **within the phase** |
| Agent | Amphiphile (lecithin, protein, gum) | Starch, gum, reduction, coagulated protein |
| Result | Creamy, homogeneous, stable texture | Sauce that coats the spoon |

A sauce can be:

- **thickened only** — a velouté, a tomato sauce, a cornflour sauce. It coats, with no emulsion involved;
- **emulsified only** — a well-built vinaigrette. Creamy, but not "thick" in the starchy sense;
- **both** — a hollandaise (an emulsion of butter stabilised by the yolk *and* thickened by that yolk's partial coagulation), a béarnaise, many rich simmered sauces.

Two independent properties, often combined but to be thought of separately.

### Starch as an indirect emulsion stabiliser

Starch is **not** an emulsifier: it has no amphiphilic head/tail structure and does not adsorb at oil-water interfaces. Its action on an emulsion is **indirect but real**: by thickening the aqueous phase, it immobilises the fat droplets, slows their encounters and therefore their coalescence. It stabilises **through viscosity, not through a film**.

That is exactly xanthan gum's role in modern cooking — and it is why a pinch of starch is the most reliable patch for a sauce that has released its fat: you restore the viscosity and re-trap the fat. See [[emulsions.en]].

### The three families of kitchen thickeners

- **Starches** (cornflour, wheat flour, potato starch, rice, arrowroot; the starch of starchy vegetables) — need heat to gelatinise, sensitive to acid and over-stirring.
- **Gums / hydrocolloids** (xanthan, gum arabic, mustard mucilage) — active cold, very stable to heat and acid, effective at tiny doses.
- **Coagulated proteins** (egg yolk in a crème anglaise, peanut protein in a mafé) — thicken by forming a network, but with a narrow window: go too far and you get lumps. See [[proteins-in-cooking.en]].

To which you can add **reduction**, which adds nothing: it concentrates by evaporation what is already there. Beware, it also unbalances the water/fat ratio and can break a pre-existing emulsion.

## Examples & analogies

- **The fishing net in the aquarium.** The water is still there, no gap has been filled — but it is caught in a mesh and no longer flows the same way. That is what thickening is.
- **Starch granules as sponges.** Cold, they are hard inert beads. Heated, they swell into sponges packed against one another, and it is that crowding that slows the flow. Over-boiling means tearing the sponges: they empty out and the sauce collapses.
- **Hollandaise, the textbook case of doing both.** The yolk plays two simultaneous, distinct roles: its lipoproteins and lecithin **emulsify** the butter, its partial coagulation **thickens**. One spoonful, two different physics.
