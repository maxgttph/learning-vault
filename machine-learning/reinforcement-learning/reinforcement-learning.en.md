# Reinforcement Learning vs Supervised Learning

## TL;DR

> Supervised learning learns from **labels** ("here is the right answer"), reinforcement learning learns from a **scalar reward** obtained by acting ("that was good / bad"). These are *learning paradigms* — they define **where the gradient signal comes from**, not the shape of the network. They are therefore **orthogonal** to architectures like the [[transformer-architecture.en|Transformer]] or the [[recurrent-networks-and-lstm.en|RNN]]: you combine them, you do not oppose them.

## Key concepts

- **Supervised learning** — learning a function $f_\theta(x)\approx y$ from known (input, right answer) pairs. A dense, direct signal.
- **Reinforcement learning** — an **agent** interacts with an **environment**: it observes a state, chooses an action, receives a reward. No "right answer" is provided.
- **Policy** $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ — the agent's strategy: the probability of choosing action $a$ in state $s$.
- **Discounted return** — the RL objective: maximise $\sum_t \gamma^t r_t$, the sum of future rewards weighted by $\gamma\in[0,1)$.
- **Credit assignment** — the problem of knowing *which* past action deserves credit (or blame) for a reward that arrives much later.
- **Exploration vs exploitation** — trying unknown actions to discover something better, vs exploiting what you already know pays. A dilemma unique to RL.
- **Paradigm ≠ architecture** — the paradigm says *where the signal comes from*, the architecture says *what shape* $f_\theta$ *has*. Independent axes.

## Deep dive

### Supervised learning: learning from an answer key

You have a **labelled** dataset, pairs of (input, right answer):

$$\mathcal{D} = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\}$$

The model learns $f_\theta(x)\approx y$ by minimising a **loss function** measuring the gap from the known truth:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(f_\theta(x_i),\, y_i\big)$$

The key characteristic is that the **signal is dense and direct**: for *every* example the right answer is known, and the gradient says precisely how to correct. This is pattern recognition — "here is the image, here is the fact that it is a cat". The gradient mechanics themselves (forward → loss → backprop → update) are detailed in [[how-neural-networks-train.en]]; supervised learning is its canonical case.

### Reinforcement learning: learning by acting

There is **no right answer provided**. An **agent** interacts with an **environment**: it observes a state $s_t$, chooses an action $a_t$, and receives a **reward** $r_t$ (a scalar, often zero) plus a new state. The formal framework is the *Markov Decision Process*:

- **State** $s_t$ — the current situation.
- **Action** $a_t$ — what the agent does.
- **Reward** $r_t$ — the numerical feedback.
- **Policy** $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ — the strategy, which is what gets learned.

The objective is not to match a label but to **maximise cumulative future reward**:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t\, r_t\right]$$

where $\gamma\in[0,1)$ is the **discount factor** arbitrating between present and future.

Three difficulties are specific to RL and absent from supervised learning:

- **Sparse and delayed feedback** — you can play 200 chess moves and only learn at the end that you lost. Which move was bad? That is the *credit assignment problem*.
- **No direct target** — nobody says "the right move was X"; you only know whether the overall outcome was good.
- **Exploration vs exploitation** — you sometimes have to try suboptimal actions to discover better ones, a trade-off that does not exist in supervised learning (where the data is fixed in advance).

### The contrast at a glance

| | Supervised | Reinforcement |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | a label, "the true answer" | a scalar reward, "good / bad" |
| Density | one target per example | often sparse, delayed |
| Data | fixed, provided in advance | generated by the agent as it acts |
| Goal | imitate | optimise a behaviour |

### The crucial point: paradigm ≠ architecture

This is the usual source of confusion. RL/supervised and Transformer/RNN are **not on the same axis**.

- **Transformer and RNN are *architectures***: ways of wiring a network, i.e. *the shape of the function* $f_\theta$. They answer "how do we represent and process the input?".
- **Supervised and reinforcement learning are *training paradigms***: ways of computing the signal that adjusts $\theta$. They answer "where does the gradient come from?".

The two axes combine freely:

```
                  Architecture (the "what")
                  Transformer / RNN / CNN / MLP
                          ▲
                          │
   Paradigm (the "how we learn")  ──┘
   Supervised / Reinforcement / Self-supervised
```

Example combinations: Transformer + supervised (a text classifier fine-tuned on labels); RNN + RL (recurrent robot controllers, the first Atari agents with memory); Transformer + RL (the **RLHF** of conversational LLMs).

### The LLM case: both paradigms stacked

The LLM is the perfect illustration of this complementarity — **the same architecture** (a transformer) from start to finish, with only the **paradigm** changing from stage to stage:

1. **Self-supervised pre-training** — predict the next word on raw text. The labels are free (they are the text itself), hence "self-supervised".
2. **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning)** — on (instruction, good answer) pairs curated by humans.
3. **RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)** — a reward signal derived from human preferences refines behaviour where no single answer exists (tone, helpfulness, safety).

The details of these three phases on the *training mechanics* side live in [[how-neural-networks-train.en]]; this sheet focuses on *why* they are distinct paradigms.

## Examples & analogies

- **The answer key vs the bicycle.** Supervised learning is learning with the answer key in front of you: for every exercise you see the exact answer. RL is learning to ride a bike: nobody gives you "the exact handlebar angle" — you fall (negative reward) or you move forward (positive), and you adjust.
- **RLHF.** When a conversational LLM becomes "helpful, harmless, honest", it is not by showing it the right answer (there is no single one), but by ranking its answers by preference and reinforcing the preferred ones — RL applied to a transformer.
