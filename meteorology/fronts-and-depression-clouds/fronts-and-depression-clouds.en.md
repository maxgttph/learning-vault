# Fronts and the Cloud Sequence of a Depression

## TL;DR

> A mid-latitude depression organises itself around a warm front (ahead) and a cold front (behind). The cloud sequence you watch drift past is not one cloud transforming into another: it is a **single tilted, continuous cloud sheet** that a point on the ground passes underneath, from its high thin tip (cirrus) to its low thick base (nimbostratus).

## Key concepts

- **Warm front** — warm air slides up a very gentle slope ($\approx 1/150$) over the cold air: slow ascent → layered **stratiform** clouds.
- **Cold front** — cold air abruptly lifts the warm air (steep slope): violent ascent → **convection**, cumulonimbus, showers.
- **Warm sector** — the mild air mass trapped between the two fronts.
- **Anvil (incus)** — the top of a cumulonimbus spread out beneath the tropopause; made of ice, it becomes leftover cirrus once the storm dies.
- **Buys-Ballot's law** — northern hemisphere, back to the wind → the low is on your left.

## Deep dive

### The warm-front sequence: a tilted sheet, not a cloud coming down

The intuitive error is to think the cirrus "descends" as it loads up with water. In reality the warm front's cloud sheet is a **tilted wedge**, fixed relative to the front. The frontal surface (the warm/cold interface) is high far ahead of the front and slopes down to the ground where the front lies. A stationary observer therefore sweeps through ever lower and thicker slices of the wedge as the front advances:

```
  far ahead ←———————————————→ front at surface
   cirrus   cirrostratus  altostratus  nimbostratus
   (~10 km)    (~8 km)      (~4 km)    (~1 km, rain)
   thin  ————————————————————————————→  thick
```

These precursor cirrus announce the depression **1 to 2 days in advance** (a halo around the sun at the cirrostratus stage). The crystals and droplets inside are constantly recreated by condensation in the rising air: a cloud is a **place** where air rises and condenses, not a fixed object. It is the *shape* of the wedge that stays stable and explains the sequence.

### The cold front: convection and the anvil

At the cold front, abrupt lifting produces cumulonimbus. The updraught climbs to the **tropopause**, hits the stable inversion, and spreads out horizontally → the **anvil**. That anvil is entirely made of ice.

Classic question: *does the anvil become the high-altitude cirrus?* A nuanced answer:
- The anvil **is** ice, like a cirrus, and **ends up as cirrus** ("orphan" cirrus spissatus / cirrostratus) once the convective body collapses.
- But it is **not** the same as the warm front's precursor cirrus: the mechanism is opposite (violent convection vs. slow gliding), and so is the role (residue after the storm vs. herald before the rain).

There are therefore **two families of cirrus**: those that announce (warm front) and those that linger (born from the anvil).

### Temperature and wind as the fronts pass (northern hemisphere)

The wind **veers** (turns clockwise) at each frontal passage:

| Stage | Wind | Weather |
|---|---|---|
| Ahead of the warm front | S / SE, strengthening | cirrus → rain approaching |
| Warm sector | SW, steady | mild, humid, low cloud |
| **Cold front passage** | **veers abruptly NW/W, gusty** | squalls, showers, thunderstorms |
| Behind the cold front | NW, cold, gusty | cumulus/Cb, showers + sunny spells |

Temperature drop behind the cold front: typically **4 to 8 °C**, up to **10–15 °C** in winter during a marked polar advection. Pressure, low in the warm sector, **rises clearly** after the cold front.

### The post-frontal sky

Behind the cold front, cold air moves over a surface that is still warmer → heated from below → unstable → **towering cumulus and scattered cumulonimbus**: intermittent showers with excellent visibility between them. It is the stable/stratiform mirror image of the warm front.

## Examples & analogies

- **The wedge you walk under**: imagine a ramp of frosted glass laid at a very gentle angle above you; walking forwards, you first pass under its thin edge (cirrus), then under its thick part near the ground (nimbostratus). The glass is not "coming down" — you are changing position underneath it.
- **Two ways to frost the ceiling**: the warm front is a long access ramp that deposits ice at the top (precursor cirrus); the cumulonimbus is an express lift that slams into the ceiling and spreads its load into a puddle (anvil → leftover cirrus).
