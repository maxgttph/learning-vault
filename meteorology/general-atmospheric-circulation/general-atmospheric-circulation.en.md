# General Atmospheric Circulation

## TL;DR

> The equator-to-pole heat imbalance, combined with the Earth's rotation, cuts the atmosphere into **three cells per hemisphere** (Hadley, Ferrel, polar). The boundaries at $\approx 30°$ and $\approx 60°$ are not set by cooling alone but above all by **conservation of angular momentum**: it is the Earth's rotation that chops the circulation into pieces.

## Key concepts

- **Hadley cell (0–30°)** — direct cell: rising at the equator, sinking near 30°.
- **Ferrel cell (30–60°)** — indirect cell, driven mechanically by its neighbours.
- **Polar cell (60–90°)** — direct: subsidence at the pole, ascent near 60°.
- **ITCZ** — intertropical convergence zone: equatorial low pressure, rainfall.
- **Angular momentum** — conserved by air moving in latitude; it sets the boundary at 30°.

## Deep dive

### Why 30°? Rotation, not (only) temperature

At the equator, peak solar energy lifts warm moist air (the ITCZ) → low pressure, rain. Aloft, that air heads polewards. **But** as it approaches the Earth's rotation axis it must conserve its **angular momentum**: its eastward speed keeps increasing (Coriolis deflects it more and more). By around 30° the air is flowing almost due east — forming the **subtropical jet** — and can no longer make progress polewards. It has also radiated its heat to space (cooled, densified). Result: it **sinks back down near 30°** → subsidence → **subtropical high pressure** (Azores high, Sahara).

The key point about the 30° boundary: it is **first and foremost rotational dynamics** (angular momentum), with cooling merely riding along. Without rotation, a single giant cell would run from equator to pole. A telling order of magnitude: air leaving the equator would reach 30° with a westerly wind of about $130\ \text{m/s}$ — unrealistic and unstable, hence the cell breaking there.

### The Ferrel cell: an indirect gear

At our latitudes (30–60°), the Ferrel cell is **not directly heated**: it turns "backwards" (thermally indirect), driven mechanically like a gear wedged between the Hadley and polar cells. On average, air sinks at 30° and rises at 60°.

More importantly, the real poleward heat transport at these latitudes is **not** carried by that mean cell but by the **eddies** = the lows and highs themselves (see [[lows-and-highs.en]]). The Ferrel cell is therefore a **statistical average** over a mess of baroclinic systems. Hence the reign of changeable weather and prevailing **westerlies**.

### The polar cell and the polar front at 60°

At the pole, dense frigid air sinks → **polar high pressure**. It flows out at the surface towards ~60°, where it meets mild air from the south: this is the **polar front**. The abrupt thermal contrast drives ascent, **subpolar low pressure** (the Icelandic low), and the birth of the **polar jet** and its depressions.

### The overall picture

| Latitude | Vertical motion | Surface pressure | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0° equator | Ascent | Low | Rain, tropical forest, ITCZ |
| 30° subtropics | **Subsidence** | **High** | Deserts, Azores high, trade winds |
| 60° subpolar | Ascent | Low | Polar front, Icelandic low, storms |
| 90° pole | Subsidence | High | Cold polar desert |

Simple rule: **30° and 90° = subsidence = high pressure = dry; 0° and 60° = ascent = low pressure = wet.**

## Examples & analogies

- **The skater's spin**: air heading polewards moves closer to the axis; like a skater pulling in their arms, it speeds up its rotation (eastward velocity) — until that becomes untenable near 30°.
- **The middle gear**: the Hadley and polar cells are two driving wheels; the Ferrel cell is the wheel wedged between them, turning backwards under their drive.
