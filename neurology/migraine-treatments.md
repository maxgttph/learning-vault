# Migraine Treatments & Pharmacology

## TL;DR

> Migraine drugs split into **preventive** (taken daily, raise the trigger threshold) and **acute** (taken during an attack, abort it). They act at different points of the cascade: propranolol and candesartan calm the excitable *terrain*; triptans, ditans, gepants and NSAIDs target the *attack* itself. The elegant pair: a preventive stops the brain lighting the fuse; an acute drug puts out the fire.

## Key concepts

- **Receptor** — a "lock" protein; a **ligand** (key) binding it triggers a cell response. Most relevant ones here are **GPCRs** (relay a chemical cascade, not an ion channel).
- **Agonist vs antagonist** — an agonist *activates* the receptor; an antagonist *blocks* it.
- **Adrenergic receptors** — respond to (nor)adrenaline; **β1** (heart), **β2** (bronchi/vessels), **α1** (vessels).
- **Beta-blocker** — adrenergic **antagonist**; non-selective ones block β1 *and* β2.
- **ISA** (intrinsic sympathomimetic activity) — partial-agonist beta-blockers; these **do not** prevent migraine.
- **5-HT receptors** — serotonin receptors; triptans target 5-HT(1B/1D), ditans 5-HT(1F).
- **CGRP pathway drugs** — gepants (block receptor) and monoclonal antibodies (neutralise CGRP/receptor).

## Deep dive

### Pharmacology primer

A **receptor** is a protein (usually membrane-bound) acting as a lock; when its **ligand** binds, a cellular response fires. The adrenergic and serotonergic receptors here are **GPCRs** — they trigger an internal chemical cascade rather than opening an ion channel.

- An **agonist** binds and *turns the lock* (mimics the natural ligand).
- An **antagonist** binds and *blocks* it (prevents the natural ligand acting).

**Adrenergic receptors** respond to noradrenaline/adrenaline (the sympathetic, fight-or-flight messengers): **β1** mostly on the heart (speeds it up), **β2** on bronchi and some vessels (dilates), **α1** on vessels (constricts). **Lipophilicity** matters: lipophilic drugs cross the **blood–brain barrier** and act centrally; hydrophilic ones stay peripheral.

### Preventive: Propranolol

A **non-selective beta-blocker** (blocks β1 *and* β2) **without ISA**, and **lipophilic** so it enters the brain — which is why it works on migraine. First-line prevention; ~$50\%$ reduction in attack frequency in many patients; takes **4–8 weeks** to judge; useless during an attack.

Mechanisms (not fully settled): ↓ central noradrenergic tone (calms cortical excitability), ↓ frequency of CSD (raises its threshold experimentally), modulation of serotonin (5-HT2), and inhibition of **NO** production — relevant because NO is an attack *initiator* (see [[migraine-pathophysiology]]), not merely a vasodilator.

**The ISA clue**: beta-blockers *with* ISA (pindolol, acebutolol — partial agonists that keep the receptor ~$20\%$ on) **do not** prevent migraine. This proves the effect needs **complete, sustained** β-blockade plus central actions, not just "being a beta-blocker".

**Contraindications**: asthma/COPD (β2 block → bronchospasm — major), severe bradycardia, AV block, decompensated heart failure; caution in diabetes (masks hypoglycaemia signs). The old propranolol–depression link is now largely refuted; reported mood effects are more likely the drug's **fatigue** mistaken for depression.

### Preventive: Candesartan

An angiotensin-II receptor **antagonist** ("sartan"), originally an antihypertensive. Like propranolol, it is a cardiovascular drug repurposed for migraine prevention — and, also like propranolol, its benefit is **not** from lowering blood pressure (not all antihypertensives work). It acts via the brain's renin–angiotensin system: ↓ neuronal excitability, ↓ inflammation/oxidative stress, ↓ sympathetic tone. A useful alternative when propranolol is contraindicated (e.g. asthma).

### Preventive: anti-excitability antiepileptics

**Topiramate** and **valproate** target the terrain directly — boosting **GABA** (the brake) and damping **glutamate** / ion channels (the accelerator). The literal "reduce hyperexcitability" strategy (see [[cortical-hyperexcitability]]).

### Acute: Triptans

**Agonists** of **5-HT(1B) and 5-HT(1D)** serotonin receptors. Three complementary effects: (1) 5-HT(1B) on meningeal vessels → **vasoconstriction**; (2) 5-HT(1D) on trigeminal endings → **blocks CGRP release** → cuts neurogenic inflammation; (3) inhibits pain transmission toward the brainstem. Take **early** in the attack. **Contraindicated** in coronary disease/stroke (the vasoconstriction would also narrow heart/brain arteries).

Note: a triptan does **not** "boost serotonin" generally — it is a *selective* key for 1B/1D receptors (on vessels and trigeminal nerves, not the mood circuits), and it barely crosses the blood–brain barrier. So no euphoria.

### Acute: Ditans

**Lasmiditan**, a **5-HT(1F)** agonist. Still serotonergic, but the 1F receptor is **not on vessels** → calms pain **without vasoconstriction** → usable in cardiovascular patients.

### Acute & preventive: anti-CGRP drugs

The first drugs *designed specifically* for migraine.

- **Gepants** (rimegepant, atogepant, ubrogepant): small molecules that **block the CGRP receptor**; acute and/or preventive; no vasoconstriction (usable in cardiac patients).
- **Monoclonal antibodies** (erenumab, galcanezumab, fremanezumab, eptinezumab): large proteins, monthly/quarterly injection, **preventive**; neutralise CGRP or its receptor.

Side effects: generally well tolerated; **constipation** (CGRP regulates gut motility), injection-site reactions, sometimes raised blood pressure. Theoretical caution: CGRP is a **protective** vasodilator during ischaemia, so long-term blockade *might* reduce that protection in high-risk patients — reassuring so far, but recent.

### Acute: NSAIDs & antiemetics

**NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, ketoprofen, aspirin) hit the **neurogenic inflammation** by blocking prostaglandins (COX enzyme) — a route independent of serotonin. **Antiemetics** (metoclopramide) relieve nausea and improve absorption of other drugs.

### Safety: serotonin syndrome

Combining a triptan with drugs that genuinely raise *global* serotonin (SSRIs/SNRIs) can cause an excess → the rare but serious **serotonin syndrome**. The one case where "more serotonin" applies — as a risk, not a benefit.

### Acute vs preventive at a glance

| | **Propranolol** | **Triptans** |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Preventive | Acute |
| Target | β-adrenergic receptors | 5-HT(1B/1D) (serotonin) |
| Action | **Antagonist** (blocks) | **Agonist** (activates) |
| Stage hit | The terrain (upstream) | The attack (downstream) |
| When | Daily, continuous | At attack onset |
| Onset of benefit | Weeks | Minutes–1–2 h |

## Examples & analogies

- **Light dimmer (ISA)**: adrenaline pushes the receptor to $100\%$; a pure antagonist blocks it to $0\%$; an ISA partial agonist holds it at ~$20\%$ — that residual glow is enough to nullify the anti-migraine benefit.
- **Specialised key (triptan)**: not a master key flooding all serotonin doors, just one cut for the 1B/1D locks tied to the attack.

## Open questions

- Long-term safety of chronic CGRP blockade in cardiovascular-risk patients.
- Selective neuronal NOS inhibitors as a future class (vessel-sparing).
